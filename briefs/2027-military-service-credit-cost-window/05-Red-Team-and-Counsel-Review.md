## 1. The strongest case against — written as the opposition would write it

> *Chairman, I want to be careful here, because nobody on this committee wants to be
> against veterans. So let me talk only about the document.*
>
> *This proposal arrives with a one-page handout that says, in bold, "General fund cost:
> $0. That is structural, not a rounding." That is not correct, and the proponents' own
> annex knows it is not correct — it books the state-agency share as "general fund plus
> nongeneral fund." Under § 51.1-145 I the Board certifies a rate, under § 51.1-145 K the
> Governor's appropriation bill must carry the resulting employer contributions, and under
> § 51.1-145 L this body sets the rate. So the general fund pays. Not this year. Next
> biennium, in the retirement line, where nobody is looking. And when VRS files its
> § 30-19.1:7 statement — a statute that by its terms requires the Board to report "the
> financial impact of the proposed bill upon the general fund" — I expect it to say what
> it said about the last bill like this one: "no immediate impact," and "Budget Amendment
> Necessary: Yes."*
>
> *Second: the handout is titled "Let veteran **state employees** buy their military
> retirement credit at a fair price." It never tells the reader that school divisions and
> local governments pay too. The proponents' own exposure table shows the
> political-subdivision line is the **largest of the three**. The Virginia Municipal
> League and VACo are going to read that table and then read that handout, and they are
> going to draw a conclusion about this organisation that has nothing to do with veterans.*
>
> *Third, and this is the one I'd ask the actuary about. Their brief says the strongest
> fiscal argument for this bill is that a veteran under 47 or 48 "pays more than the
> credit is actuarially worth" — the veteran subsidises the Fund. Then, three pages later,
> the same brief says every purchaser costs the Fund money: $6,779 for a Hybrid member,
> and nearly every Hybrid member in the Commonwealth is under 47. Both statements cannot
> be true. I ran their own model. On the basis their cost table actually uses, the Fund
> loses money on every purchaser at every age from 28 to 61, in all three plans. So one of
> those two numbers is being used to sell the bill and the other is being used to price
> it, and they contradict each other.*
>
> *Fourth: they call the HB 1867 comparison "the cross-check that matters most" and "the
> strongest available evidence that this estimate is in the right range." HB 1867 gave the
> service away **free**, for **four years**. VRS priced that at $10,000 to $60,000 a
> member, average $35,000. The proponents compare that to a figure that is net of what the
> veteran pays, for two and a half years. Put it on the same footing — free, four years —
> and their Plan 1 number is **$111,680**. That is 1.9 times the top of VRS's entire range
> and more than three times VRS's own average. Their cross-check, run correctly, says
> their estimate is hot, not conservative.*
>
> *Fifth: the bill's answer to buying on the eve of retirement is a Board power to require
> that a purchase be "completed not fewer than 12 months prior to the member's effective
> date of retirement." A member has no effective date of retirement when they buy. And the
> very next amendment in the same draft begins "Notwithstanding any other provision of this
> section" and authorises sixty-month payment agreements. So the guardrail is overridden by
> the convenience, in the same bill, on the same page.*
>
> *Last: who is actually helped. § 51.1-142.2 B 3 says no service credit may be purchased
> "if it is included in the calculation of any retirement allowance received or to be
> received… or if there is a balance in a defined contribution account that serves as a
> primary retirement account related to such service." A twenty-year military retiree
> cannot buy. A soldier who served under the Blended Retirement System has a TSP account
> funded by the government against that very service. The proponents' estimate of "10,600
> to 26,300 veterans" is built from Census and DHRM counts of **veterans**, and it applies
> a haircut for the 180-day rule and for nothing else. Their own package does not mention
> either half of that sentence — not in the brief, not in the annexes, not in the eight-item
> data request. I'd want VRS to tell us how many of those 26,300 are statutorily barred
> before I vote on a number.*

That brief is survivable. Every one of those six points has a fix, four of them are one
sentence long, and the underlying policy argument — that Virginia withdraws a price from a
veteran for the offence of staying — is strong and survives all of it. But as the package
is printed today, the opposition does not have to argue about veterans at all. It can
argue about arithmetic and about a handout, and it will win that argument.

## 2. Findings, ranked by severity

**Fatal** = this defeats the document or the provision it sits in.
**Serious** = a hostile reader wins an exchange on it.
**Moderate** = it costs credibility and is cheap to fix.
**Minor** = tidy it before filing.

## 4. Fiscal assumption challenge

### 4.1 The load-bearing assumption is not the one the brief names

The brief names **take-up**: "the single input that would most narrow this range," "roughly 6 of that 14 is take-up alone." That is true of the *published* range, and it is a fair thing to say. But take-up is a scenario dial — it is honestly presented as a grid and nobody is misled by it.

The assumption the conclusion actually rests on, and which carries **no band at all**, is the **representative purchaser profile**. The per-member net present values — $47,967 / $17,174 / $6,779 — are single-profile point estimates. The Plan 1 representative purchaser is **age 55 with 25 years of service**, which is exactly the profile `04` FM-2 § 2.4 calls "a long-service member sitting just short of an eligibility threshold, which is the **worst case, not the typical one**." The brief rejects that profile as unrepresentative when defending the chart, and adopts it when pricing the bill.

**The test.** Hold the model, the salary basis and the 2.5 purchased years constant; vary only age and service.

| Plan | Profile range tested | Net PV per purchaser | Spread |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plan 1 | age 45 / 16 yrs → age 60 / 29 yrs | $28,677 → $57,277 | **2.0×** |
| Plan 2 | age 45 / 12 yrs → age 58 / 20 yrs | $14,359 → $26,030 | 1.8× |
| Hybrid | age 38 / 5 yrs → age 50 / 9 yrs | $3,959 → $6,779 | 1.7× |

**The result.** Profile mix is a third source of variance of the same order as the pool band (2.3×), and the brief attributes the entire range to pool and take-up. If the average purchaser is ten years younger than the representative profile — which is what the brief's own recruitment story implies, with the average new state hire at 37.7 — the headline falls by roughly 40%. The honest headline is therefore **wider at the bottom than published**, and the fix is cheap: run each plan at three profiles and publish the per-member figure as a band.

## 5. Counsel questions LC-1 to LC-4 — closed

### LC-1. Does failure to give notice toll the 24-month window, or is the duty directive only?

**Recommendation: directive only — and recognise first that the question is now largely moot.**

Three steps.

1. **Moot as to military service.** The bill *removes* the deadline for subdivision B 2 purchases. There is no window left to toll. Removing a deadline is a strictly better remedy for the veteran than requiring notice of it, and it cannot be scored as a retrospective liability. The notice recommendation that was Recommendation B in v1.0 has silently disappeared from `01` v1.2; whichever way JLCVSO decides, **say so on the face of the brief** (M-4).

2. **If a general notice duty is nonetheless added** for the B 1 windows the bill leaves alone, draft it as **directive**. Two reasons, and the first is doctrinal rather than tactical. Under *Jamborsky v. Baskins*, 247 Va. 506, 442 S.E.2d 636 (1994), "the use of the word *shall* in a statute requiring action by a public official is directory and not mandatory unless the statute manifests a contrary intent." A bare "the Retirement System or the employer shall give notice" will therefore be construed as directive **anyway**; a tolling remedy must be written expressly and conspicuously or it does not exist. The choice is not between a strong duty and a weak one — it is between a duty whose consequence is stated and one whose consequence is zero.

3. **Why not to write the tolling remedy.** It is an open-ended retrospective liability of a kind VRS cannot price and will oppose on that ground alone. It re-opens the normal-cost price for an indeterminate number of members for whom no employer can now prove notice, across 864 participating employers, with no record-keeping requirement that ever applied. That is the second bill with the second score, and it is the one that does not pass. It also puts the proponent in the position of asking the General Assembly to create a cause of action against school divisions and localities in the same session it is asking them to absorb a rate increase.

**If JLCVSO wants the duty to bite without the tail**, there is a bounded middle: notice at hire and again at month 18, employer certification retained in the member record, and a remedy limited to a **fixed 12-month extension** available only to members **first hired after the effective date**. Prospective, bounded, countable, priceable. That is the version an actuary can score and a locality can live with.

### LC-2. Does an earlier unreduced-eligibility date reached partly through purchased credit disturb the PLOP active-service requirement?

**Confirmed: no.** § 51.1-165.01 B establishes three tiers, each requiring **both** creditable service *performed* after the date the member first becomes eligible for an unreduced service retirement allowance **and** being "in service" for 1, 2 or 3 full years after January 1, 2001. Purchased military service is **prior service** — "service rendered prior to becoming a member of the Retirement System" (§ 51.1-124.3) — so it is not *performed* after the eligibility date and cannot satisfy either gate. The requirement for actual worked service is undisturbed. Annex A § 5's reading is correct.

Two things to carry forward.

- **A volume effect that is not priced.** Moving the first-eligibility date earlier starts the post-eligibility clock sooner, so a member who keeps working can accumulate the 3 years needed for the largest PLOP tier where previously they could reach only 1 or 2. More members reach higher tiers. PLOP is designed to be actuarially neutral, so this should be close to cost-free — but it is a utilisation change VRS will want to see named, and it is absent from `04` § 4.9.
- **Enactment clause 2 (v) is drafted too wide and may defeat the very acceleration the bill intends.** See M-7, with the narrowing language.

### LC-3. Is the two-year payment-agreement rule administrative rather than statutorily compelled?

**View: administrative. The statute does not compel it, and relaxing it delivers a real part of the objective with no bill — but a smaller part than the brief implies.**

The textual case is strong and rests on two verified provisions.

- **§ 51.1-142.2 B 3** conditions the price on the member "purchas[ing], **or enter[ing] into a purchase of service credit contract for**," the service within 24 months. The statutory event is *entry into the contract*, not its completion. On the statute's own words a contract entered into in month 23 and performed over the following four years is priced at approximate normal cost.
- **§ 51.1-142.2 F** is the payment provision and contains **no duration limit whatsoever**: "Payment may be made in a lump sum at the time of purchase or by payroll deduction. **Any number of additional deductions may be permitted at any time.**" It expressly contemplates partial performance: "Should any deduction be terminated before the member purchases the entire period contracted for, the member shall be credited with the number of full or partial months of service for which full payment has been made." A statute that provides a rule for *incomplete* payment is not a statute that requires completion inside 24 months.

Both restrictions — the 6-to-12-month duration and "**[t]he duration of an agreement may not extend beyond the two-year approximate normal cost window**" — appear on VRS's *Payment Methods* page, verified verbatim. Neither appears in the Code.

**The limit of the relief, stated plainly so nobody oversells it.** Relaxing the rule helps **only members still inside the 24-month window**: it lets a new veteran hire spread $18,666 over five years instead of twelve months. It does nothing for the 110,474 Plan 1 members already past the window, because after month 24 the price is actuarial equivalent cost and no payment term restores the normal-cost price. So LC-3 is a genuine bill-free win for the **in-window** population — and it means **Fallback 1 is only half administrable.** Payment flexibility for in-window members needs no bill; payment flexibility at any career stage still does, because the thing that needs changing is the price, not the schedule. Correct `01`'s Fallback 1 accordingly.

### LC-4. Reach — are current members already past month 24 in or out?

**In, and the draft achieves it.** Annex A § 2.2 prices B 2 purchases at approximate normal cost "**without regard to the time elapsed since his first date of hire or the final day of any applicable leave of absence**." That language forecloses a prospective-only construction. The July 1, 2027 effective date in enactment clause 4 raises no retroactivity question, because the act changes the price of a **future** purchase and does not reprice a completed one; it is prospective in operation even though it reaches members hired long ago. Clause 4 is also redundant against the default effective date and may be struck on DLS advice without affecting reach.

**Three limits the brief should state, because "at any point in their career" is not quite what the bill delivers.**

1. **"Any member in service."** That is § 51.1-142.2 B 2's own language and the draft carries it forward, so the right ends at separation: **deferred members** (separated, contributions not withdrawn) and **retirees** are out. VRS said exactly this about the analogous grant in its HB 1867 statement — a member is ineligible "if they are employed in a non-covered position, on a leave of absence without pay, a deferred member or a retiree." A veteran who leaves state service at 55 intending to buy later cannot. If JLCVSO wants deferred members reached, that is a separate and larger amendment with its own score; if not, the brief should stop saying "at any point in their career" without qualification.
2. **The two anti-duplication limbs** (S-4) cut into the reached population regardless of the window.
3. **Veterans who already paid actuarial equivalent cost get nothing** (S-7).

## 7. Constitutional and preemption screen — mostly null, and the nulls are worth stating

| Lens | Result |
|---|---|
| **Single object** (Va. Const. art. IV, § 12) | **No issue.** One section amended; the title states the object. |
| **Special legislation** (Va. Const. art. IV, § 14) | **No issue.** A statewide classification within a statewide system, applicable by rule to all members meeting the criteria. Not local, special or private. |
| **Delegation without standards** | **No issue, and this was handled well.** The Board's discretion over "approximate normal cost" is pre-existing ("as determined by the Board in its sole discretion"). The risk Annex A § 8.4 identifies is the real one — that an age-graded schedule might be argued to exceed an "approximate normal cost" mandate — and express authority plus the publication, uniformity and AEC-ceiling constraints cure it. The decision to use a **closed** factor list rather than a residual clause is the right one for exactly the reason the drafting note gives. |
| **Age discrimination** (ADEA, 29 U.S.C. § 623) — Annex A Q14 | **Low risk, and v1.1 reduced it. I regard Q14 as substantially answerable now.** Two grounds. (1) The **equal benefit or equal cost** standard, 29 U.S.C. § 623(f)(2)(B)(i) (OWBPA), permits observing the terms of a bona fide employee benefit plan where the cost incurred on behalf of an older worker is no less than that incurred for a younger one — which is precisely what a cost-justified graded schedule does, and the **actuarial-equivalent-cost ceiling is what keeps the differential cost-justified**. Draft the schedule so the Board's record shows it tracks cost; a crude band adopted to hit a fiscal target is the exposed version. (2) *Hazen Paper Co. v. Biggins*, 507 U.S. 604, 611 (1993) — age and years of service are analytically distinct, so an employer may take account of one while ignoring the other. Grading on **creditable service** or **distance from unreduced eligibility** is therefore not facially age-based, which is a further reason the v1.1 widening *reduces* exposure rather than creating it. Annex A § 2.5's instinct is right; it simply cites nothing. Add these two authorities. |
| **Contract Clause / impairment** (Va. Const. art. I, § 11) | **No issue.** A benefit expansion impairs nothing. The one place to watch is S-7: if a make-whole for prior actuarial-cost purchasers is added, draft it as a gratuitous legislative adjustment, not as a remedy for a past wrong. |
| **Takings, vagueness, compelled speech, First Amendment** | **No issue.** Nothing compels speech, takes property, or turns on content. The one vagueness-adjacent item is factor (ii)'s indeterminacy (M-9), which is a uniformity problem, not a constitutional one. |
| **Dillon's Rule** | **No issue, and the lens points the other way.** The bill imposes no duty on a locality requiring a grant of power; it changes a cost that flows through § 51.1-145 employer rates for localities that have elected to participate under § 51.1-800. The General Assembly plainly may do this. The exposure is **political, not legal** — which is why it belongs in the brief's strategy, not its legal annex. |
| **Federal preemption** | **No issue.** Governmental plans are exempt from ERISA's substantive provisions. Two federal interactions are correctly flagged as open: IRC **§ 415(n)** permissive service credit and annual additions limits (Q6), which the 60-month term and later-career purchases press on; and **10 U.S.C. ch. 1223**, preserved verbatim (Q7). **Add a third:** **USERRA** (38 U.S.C. ch. 43) and its interaction with § 51.1-142.2 **I**. The bill leaves subsection I unamended, which is correct, but once B 2 is repriced it becomes materially cheaper for some members to use I than B 2 or vice versa, and the two provisions serve adjacent populations. Confirm with benefits counsel that no member is worse off, and that nothing in the bill can be read to narrow a USERRA-protected reemployment right. |
| **Litigation surface** | Thin, and worth saying so. The most plausible claim is an ADEA or Virginia Human Rights Act challenge to a graded rate schedule by a member charged a higher band — plaintiff: a member aged 58 charged more than a member aged 42 for identical credit; venue: EDVA or a Virginia circuit court; defence: § 623(f)(2)(B)(i) equal-cost, supported by the AEC ceiling and the Board's actuarial record. Next most plausible: a member denied PLOP under enactment clause 2 (v) (M-7), in a Virginia circuit court on the clause's plain words. Both are drafting-curable, and both are cured by the fixes above. |

## 9. What I checked and found sound — explicitly

Stated because a review that reports only defects is not a review.

- **The § 51.1-124.3 hinge.** Verified verbatim. The definition names § 51.1-142.2 by number; the draft amends that section rather than creating a new one; the whole downstream analysis therefore holds. The warning not to use a new Code section is correct and is the single most valuable sentence in the package.
- **The statutory quotations.** Every verbatim quotation of § 51.1-142.2 B 2, B 3, F and I, the 180-consecutive-day definition, and § 51.1-124.3's four definitions is accurate. That is unusual and it matters.
- **The narrow-bill architecture.** Carving out subdivision B 2 by express cross-reference rather than deleting the 24-month sentence is right, and Annex A § 4 is correct that the sentence serves both B 1 and B 2. Leaving subsection E, subsection I, both four-year caps, the anti-duplication sentence and the ch. 1223 exception alone is right. Annex A § 8's ten drafting points are, with the exceptions in § 6 above, well drawn.
- **The unintended-effects check.** Annex A § 5 is thorough and its governing insight — that because purchased military service is already creditable service, nothing changes *in kind* and several things change *in volume* — is correct and is the right organising principle. The AFC analysis is right (purchase payments are not creditable compensation). The Hybrid disability point is right and well spotted: § 51.1-169 D 2 means the dominant post-2014 cohort is unaffected.
- **The Rule of 90 half-year asymmetry.** Correct, mechanically sound, independently reproduced in the model, and genuinely the most important technical fact in the package. Keep it prominent; just fix the subdivision cite (M-1).
- **FM-5.** Closed properly. The page-by-page reading of the rate charts — one page re-stamped July 1, 2026 and three not, with PDF metadata to match — is exactly how a negative should be closed, and "the rates hold through at least June 30, 2029" is a real and quotable asset.
- **FM-3.** Fully sourced. The structural finding that there is no Hybrid hazardous-duty rate because § 51.1-169 A excludes those members, so Plan 2 is not a closed cohort, is correct, verified, and was a live error in v1.0. Annex A § 2.6's resolution of the 10.01% alternate option through factor (v), anchored on § 51.1-138 because § 51.1-169 A already uses that phrase, is good drafting — borrowed usage rather than invented.
- **The guardrail dial analysis (FM-4 § 4.8).** The best analytical work in the package. The finding that age-grading removes ~15% while distance-from-eligibility grading can remove up to 100%, and the reason — acceleration is driven by proximity to a threshold, which is age *and* service — is correct, consequential, and was correctly escalated and acted on.
- **The 110,474 Plan 1 statement.** Holds. It is a legal consequence of the plan rules, not an estimate, the two qualifications are correctly stated, and the "floor" characterisation is right. It survives every finding above, including S-4 — that finding goes to who may *purchase*, not to who is past the window. Lead with it.
- **The retraction discipline.** The handling of the $2 million figure — retracted everywhere, with the reason recorded so the retraction can be defended rather than merely asserted, and the non-duplication rule between Annex B and Annex 2 Closure so the two cannot diverge — is better practice than most legislative packages manage. The pairing error at S-3 is a real defect in one line; it is not a pattern.
- **Cost-driver identification.** That retirement-date acceleration and not the price discount is the dominant channel is correct, and the refinement in `04` § 4.2 — that this is true for the long-service Plan 1 cohort and an overstatement for a 45-year-old Hybrid member — is more honest than the original claim.
- **"What to say / what not to say."** This device appears throughout and it is the right instinct for a document going to busy staff. Keep it, and extend it to the general-fund sentence and the HB 1867 comparison.
