## OBJECTIVE

Amend the Virginia Retirement System so that **a veteran working for Virginia who served on active duty before being hired can buy up to four years of military service credit at the standard "normal cost" price at any point in their career** — instead of only during their first 24 months on the job.

Today that price disappears 24 months after a veteran's first day of work. After that the veteran pays a much higher "actuarial" price for the exact same credit.

---

## DISCUSSION

### What veterans can do today

A state employee who served at least 180 consecutive days on active duty, and whose discharge was not dishonorable, may buy up to four years of military service credit toward their VRS pension (§ 51.1-142.2 B 2). That credit is real: it counts toward retiring, toward the size of the pension check, and toward the retiree health insurance credit.

### Where the unfairness is

**The price has an expiration date, and it expires before most veterans can afford it.**

The law says a member pays the "approximate normal cost" — a flat percentage of salary set by the VRS Board. But if the member does not buy, or sign a payment contract, **within the first 24 months of active service following his first date of hire**, the member must instead pay the "actuarial equivalent cost" (§ 51.1-142.2 B 3).

That creates a cliff. Here is what it costs a veteran to buy the full four years at the normal-cost price, using VRS's published rates and the average classified state salary of $69,865:

| Plan the veteran is in | Normal-cost rate, per year of credit | Full 4-year purchase |
|---|---|---|
| Hybrid Retirement Plan (most members hired since Jan. 1, 2014) | 6.68% of salary | **$18,666** |
| VRS Plan 2 (hired July 2010 – Dec. 2013, **and still open — see below**) | 10.74% of salary | $30,017 |
| VRS Plan 1 (longest-serving members; closed to hires after June 30, 2010) | 12.50% of salary | $34,933 |

Hazardous-duty members pay roughly double: **23.78%** in Plan 1 and **19.15%** in Plan 2, with a separate **10.01%** alternate hazardous duty option for localities that have not adopted the full enhanced benefits. Judicial rates run from 19.21% to 31.97%. (All rates: VRS Board, Request for Board Action 2025-06-10, effective July 1, 2025. Full table at Annex 2 Closure, FM-3.)

**Plan 2 is not a closed cohort, and the brief said so in error through v1.1.** VRS ACFR FY2025, Figure 2.10, is explicit: members covered under VaLORS, SPORS, or VRS with enhanced hazardous duty benefits or the hazardous duty alternate option are in Plan 2 "even if their membership dates are after December 31, 2013." Plan 2 therefore keeps admitting new members today — State Police officers, Virginia law officers, and political-subdivision hazardous duty employees — because § 51.1-169 A excludes hazardous duty members from the Hybrid plan entirely. And from **July 1, 2026**, newly appointed judges enter Plan 2 as well (2026 Acts of Assembly, SB 950). The fiscal note must contemplate a continuing inflow of Plan 2 hazardous-duty purchasers at the 19.15% rate, not a shrinking closed group.

**The rates quoted here will not go stale during the 2027 session.** Approximate normal cost rates are reset only on the quadrennial cycle, following the Board's acceptance of a new experience study; the Board took no rate action in 2026, and VRS staff told the Benefits and Actuarial Committee on June 17, 2026 that the next assumption review is **2029**. The July 1, 2025 rates are therefore expected to hold through at least **June 30, 2029**.

Three things make that 24-month deadline the wrong deadline:

1. **It lands at the worst possible moment financially.** Veterans come to state service mid-career — the average new state hire is **37.7 years old**. A veteran who just left the military is settling a family, often taking a pay cut, and is at the *bottom* of the state pay scale. Asking for $18,666 in the first two years is asking at the one moment they cannot pay.

2. **Veterans cannot even spread the payments out past the cliff.** VRS payment agreements run 6 to 12 months and, by rule, **may not extend beyond the two-year cost window**. So a veteran cannot simply pay it off slowly. They must find the money fast or lose the price permanently.

3. **Almost nobody knows about it in time.** This is one line buried in a long purchase-of-service statute, explained during onboarding alongside dozens of other forms. By the time a veteran understands what their military service is worth to their pension, the window is shut. The average classified state employee has **10.3 years of service** — five times longer than the window.

The result: a veteran who served four years in the Army and has worked for Virginia for eight years pays dramatically more for the same four years of credit than a brand-new colleague who served the same four years. **Same service to the country, same service credit, different price — and the only difference is how long they have been loyal to the Commonwealth.**

### Who is affected, and how many

**Start with the one number that needs no estimate at all.**

> **All 110,474 active VRS Plan 1 members — 29.2% of the entire VRS active population of 378,060 — are past the 24-month window as a matter of law.**

That follows arithmetically from the plan rules: a Plan 1 membership date must precede July 1, 2010 (VRS ACFR FY2025, Figure 2.10), and the window closes 24 months after the first date of hire. It requires no survey, no proxy, and no VRS data run. Two small qualifications, both of which should be stated: a member returning from an eligible leave of absence can hold a fresh window (§ 51.1-142.2 B 3), and the window runs on *active* service, which can only push members later out of the window, never earlier. The second makes 110,474 a **floor**.

**Now the part that does require estimation: how many of those members hold military service they could buy.**

No published source reports VRS members by military-service status, and VRS itself can only identify members who have *already applied* — a purchase requires a DD-214 or service orders. Members who hold the service and never applied are visible only through employer self-identification. So the veteran share has to come from outside VRS, and v1.1's single 6% figure was applied far too broadly. It is sound for state classified employees and simply wrong for teachers.

| Group | Active members | Veteran share | Basis for the share |
|---|---:|---|---|
| VRS State Employees | 83,148 | 6.0% – 9.9% | DHRM self-identified, 6/30/2024 (OFFICIAL, and a floor because it is self-reported); upper bound is the BLS benchmark × Virginia uplift |
| VRS Teachers | 160,800 | 2.3% – 5.4% | NCES *Digest of Education Statistics* table 209.27: **2.3% of U.S. public school teachers are veterans**, 3.1% in the South |
| VRS Political Subdivision Employees | 124,264 | 5.7% – 9.9% | BLS state-and-local benchmark; public-safety-heavy, so likely in the upper half |
| SPORS / VaLORS | 9,378 | 9.9% – 25.0% | Estimate. The weakest band in the package — sworn law enforcement recruits heavily from the military, but no published Virginia figure exists |
| JRS | 470 | 2.3% – 9.9% | Estimate, bracketed by the teacher floor and the public-sector ceiling |
| **Total** | **378,060** | | |

**One independent check is worth quoting to the money committees.** Derived from BLS, *Employment Situation of Veterans*, Table 8 (August 2025): veterans are 12.9% of 7,893,000 employed veterans in state and local government and nonveterans 10.9% of 153,213,000 — which puts the veteran share of all U.S. state-and-local-government workers at **5.75%**. A national household survey and a Virginia self-identification field, two wholly unrelated instruments, land within a quarter of a percentage point of each other. DHRM's 6% is corroborated.

Carrying those bands against the members who are past the window — vesting is five years (§ 51.1-153), so every vested member is necessarily past a 24-month window, and the only uncertainty is how the 139,829 non-vested members distribute across years 0–5 — gives:

| | Low | High |
|---|---:|---:|
| Members past the 24-month window | 294,163 (77.8%) | 322,128 (85.2%) |
| **Of those, veterans holding purchasable service** | **about 10,600** | **about 26,300** |

(Derivation and the plan-by-plan split at Annex 2 Closure, FM-1 §§ 1.4–1.6. Each row applies a 90% factor for the 180-consecutive-day test of § 51.1-142.2 B 2.)

> **What to say to a legislator:** "Roughly ten to twenty-six thousand members across the three plans — and over a hundred and ten thousand Plan 1 members are past the deadline as a matter of law. VRS can give you the exact count of those holding verified military service; nobody else can."
>
> **What not to say:** any single number.

For reference, the figures v1.1 used — 56,917 classified state employees, 6% veterans, about 3,400 veterans, roughly 600 new veteran hires a year (DHRM, 6/30/2024) — remain correct **for the state classified workforce**. They are simply one-sixth of the population this bill now covers.

This also directly serves a goal the Commonwealth already set for itself. Virginia gives veterans a hiring preference for state jobs (§ 2.2-2903) and runs the Virginia Values Veterans program to recruit them. It is inconsistent to recruit veterans into state service and then charge them a penalty price on the one benefit that recognizes the service we recruited them for.

### Why this version of the fix, and not a bigger one

This fix is deliberately small. It **does not create any new benefit**. Veterans can already buy this credit, and the credit already counts the way ordinary service credit counts. The bill changes **only the price and the payment schedule** — one sentence. Nothing else in VRS moves: not vesting, not the pension formula, not PLOP, not disability, not survivor benefits. (The full check is in Annex A.)

That matters politically: a bill that changes a price is far easier to pass, and far easier for VRS to administer, than a bill that invents a new kind of service credit.

## COST

### The short answer

**No general fund appropriation is needed, and none is requested.** The cost lands in the employer contribution rate VRS already certifies, and is amortised there under the VRS Funding Policy. For political subdivisions it lands in their own § 51.1-145 rates. That is the same mechanism VRS uses for every other liability it carries, and it is unaffected by anything below.

**The pension liability itself is real and it is not small.** Modelled as a one-time present value for recommendation A-3 (keep the published normal-cost price; remove the deadline):

**$13 million to $182 million**, depending on how large the eligible pool turns out to be and how many members actually buy — which is **0.05% to 0.68% of VRS covered payroll**, or between 5 cents and 68 cents out of every $100 of payroll.

> **Correction to v1.1, stated plainly because the money committees will find it otherwise.** Brief v1.1 said "under $2 million one-time, under $100,000 per year ongoing." That figure should not be used. It was not a calculation error — it was internally consistent for what it covered — but it covered only the state classified workforce (about 2,600 people against a pool of 10,600–26,300) and only the price-discount channel, leaving out retirement-date acceleration, which is 20%–49% of the cost. Carried to this bill's actual scope and with acceleration priced, the per-member figure moves from about $3,750 to $6,779–$47,967. **Do not quote the $2 million figure.**

Prior-service purchase take-up is generally low because members must fund it from pay; if VRS's actual § 51.1-142.2 experience is in the low single digits, the honest headline is the bottom row.

### The cross-check that matters most

**VRS has already priced a more generous version of this bill, and our figures come in below it.**

In 2023, VRS filed a § 30-19.1:7 fiscal impact statement on **HB 1867**, which would have granted active duty military service to teachers **free of charge**. VRS found **$10,000 to $60,000 of unfunded liability per participating member.**

| | Net present value per member | Against VRS's own HB 1867 band |
|---|---:|---|
| Plan 1 | $47,967 | **inside the band** |
| Plan 2 | $17,174 | **inside the band** |
| Hybrid | $6,779 | below it — as a *priced* purchase should be |
| *Brief v1.1* | *~$3,750* | *an order of magnitude below the floor* |

A purchase at which the member still pays must cost the Fund **less** than a free grant of the same service. These figures do exactly that: two of three sit inside VRS's own band and the third sits just below it. That is the expected pattern, and it is the strongest available evidence that this estimate is in the right range rather than optimistic.

### Where the cost actually comes from

A purchase does two separate things. It raises the pension **amount**, and because purchased military service is creditable service (§ 51.1-124.3), it also moves the retirement **eligibility date** earlier. VRS's own definition of actuarial equivalent cost covers both — *"the total value of the increase in the employee's future retirement benefit **or earlier retirement eligibility date**."*

Acceleration is **20% to 49%** of the total cost, and that is a floor, not a ceiling: VRS's Senior Staff Actuary told the Benefits and Actuarial Committee on June 17, 2026 that VRS uses fixed-rate rather than truly actuarially equivalent early-retirement reductions, which "does provide a subsidy that varies by age and service." Reaching an unreduced date earlier also extinguishes that reduction. That second-order effect is not priced here and runs upward only.

**The mechanical asymmetry the JLC needs to understand, which appeared nowhere in v1.1:**

> Under Plan 1's **age 50 with 30 years** threshold (§ 51.1-153 B 1), once a member is past 50 the service leg binds alone, so **one purchased year advances retirement eligibility by one full year**.
>
> Under the **Rule of 90** (§ 51.1-153 B 3, Plan 2 and Hybrid), a year actually worked adds two points — one of age and one of service. A purchased year adds only the service point, because purchased credit is creditable service but it is not time lived. So **one purchased year advances Rule-of-90 eligibility by only half a year**.

That halving is why Plan 2 and Hybrid acceleration exposure is roughly a third of Plan 1's rather than equal to it, and it is the honest answer to the first question an actuary will ask.

### Why the per-member cost is far smaller for younger members

VRS's approximate normal cost rate is defined as "the average cost of one year of VRS service credit" — by construction, the right actuarial price at the plan's *average* member. Away from that average the price is wrong in a predictable direction, and for younger members it is wrong in the Commonwealth's favour. On the benefit-amount channel:

| Age at purchase | Plan 1 (ANC 12.50%) | Plan 2 (ANC 10.74%) | Hybrid (ANC 6.68%) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 35 | 0.66× | 0.64× | 0.62× |
| 45 | 0.94× | 0.92× | 0.89× |
| 55 | 1.35× | 1.31× | 1.28× |
| **Break-even age** | **46.6** | **47.4** | **48.2** |

A multiple below 1.0× means the veteran is paying **more** than the credit is actuarially worth. The break-even clusters tightly at **age 47–48 in all three plans**. Below that age the veteran subsidises the Fund. That is the strongest fiscal argument this proposal has, and it is why the Hybrid per-member figure is only $6,779.

**A caution about the Cliff Graph.** The 3.23× jump in the Cliff Graph is defensible — reproduced independently at 3.54× for Plan 1 at the chart's own illustrative profile (age 55, 25 years, $91,700) once eligibility acceleration is included. But that profile is a long-service member sitting just short of an eligibility threshold, which is the **worst case, not the typical one**. Across ages the multiple ranges from about **0.5× to about 3.5×**. A single-profile chart reads as universal, and it should not. *The chart should be reissued with a second panel plotting the multiple against purchase age, with the 47–48 break-even line marked.*

### What is not priced here

All of these run upward, and all should be put to VRS:

- the retiree health insurance credit, including its 15-year step function (§ 51.1-1400);
- adverse selection, addressed by the guardrails below but not quantified;
- the early-retirement reduction subsidy described above;
- VRS administrative cost — HB 1867's analogue was about $78,000 one-time;
- employer-side payroll and backfill consequences of earlier retirement. These are a cost to agencies and school divisions, not to the Fund, and **must not be netted against the pension figures**.

> **These figures are JLCVSO analyst estimates, not VRS numbers.** They use VRS's published normal-cost rates, VRS's 6.75% assumed return, the Board-adopted COLA assumptions (2.50% Plan 1, 2.25% Plan 2 and Hybrid), the 1.0% / 1.65% / 1.70% multipliers, and official VRS membership and payroll data. The full derivation, every input tagged OFFICIAL / DERIVED / ESTIMATE, is in **Annex 2 Closure**, and the model is reproducible at `models/fm_model.py`. **VRS actuaries will produce the official number** — under § 30-19.1:7 the VRS Board must file an impact statement on any VRS bill. JLCVSO should ask VRS for that estimate *before* filing. Annex 2 Closure, FM-1 § 1.7, contains a drafted data request ready to send.

### The one real fiscal risk, and how the draft handles it

If the price never expires, a member could wait until the eve of retirement and buy credit only when it is obviously profitable — for example, to cross the Rule of 90 or to reach the 15-year threshold for the retiree health insurance credit. That is called adverse selection and it is the objection VRS is most likely to raise.

The draft bill in Annex A answers it directly, with two guardrails that cost veterans nothing:

- **The Board may require any military purchase to be completed at least 12 months before the member's effective date of retirement.** No eve-of-retirement purchases.
- **The Board may set the normal-cost rate for military purchases as a schedule that varies by the member's age.** An older member pays a somewhat higher rate — still a published, predictable, affordable rate rather than a bespoke actuarial bill.

**A point of accuracy about the actuarial price.** VRS **does not publish** the actuarial equivalent cost rate: the published *Purchase of Prior Service Cost Rates* charts say only "Actuarial Cost" in every post-window cell, with no factor table, no age grading and no worked example. But the rate basis exists. The Board's own minutes of June 18, 2025 record that "the **actuarial equivalent cost rates are developed by the Plan Actuary** based on the assumptions and demographic data from the prior year valuations" — rates, plural, refreshed annually by Gabriel, Roeder, Smith & Company. So this is a **requestable document**, not a per-member computation that would have to be commissioned, and JLCVSO should ask for it by that description.

## RECOMMENDATION

**Amend and reenact § 51.1-142.2 of the Code of Virginia to do three things:**

1. **Remove the 24-month cost deadline for prior active duty military service only.** A veteran may purchase up to four years of military service credit at the approximate normal cost at any time while in service. (Leave the 24-month rule in place for every other category of purchasable service — educational leave, FMLA leave, out-of-state service, and the rest. This keeps the bill narrow and the fiscal note small.)

2. **Let veterans pay over up to 60 months** through payroll deduction, instead of being capped at 12 months inside a closing window. This fixes the cash-flow problem that is the real barrier, and it costs the retirement system nothing.

3. **Give the VRS Board two guardrails** against eve-of-retirement purchases: authority to set an age-graded normal-cost schedule for military purchases, and authority to require that a purchase be completed at least 12 months before retirement.

**No regulatory change is required.** The pricing rule is in statute, so it takes a bill. Once the statute is amended, the VRS Board already has authority to set the applicable normal-cost rate "in its sole discretion," so VRS can implement the rate schedule administratively.

Draft bill language is in **Annex A**, ready for the Division of Legislative Services.

### Which price should replace the cliff — a decision the JLC should take knowingly

Removing the deadline leaves open what the veteran then pays. Three structures were modelled (Annex 2 Closure, FM-4 § 4.3). They differ **only in the member's price**, never in the value of the credit, so net cost to the Fund is simply benefit value minus member price.

| Structure | How the price is set | Where it comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **A-1** | Combined member + employer normal cost rate at entry, applied to early-career pay, carried forward at a **statutory interest rate** | Pennsylvania model |
| **A-2** | A flat percentage of recent compensation — 6% in the source statute | New York model (N.Y. Ret. & Soc. Sec. Law § 1000) |
| **A-3** | The published **approximate normal cost** rate for the member's plan and rate group | Virginia's existing § 51.1-142.2 price. **This is what the draft bill does.** |

**A-1's entire character turns on the statutory interest rate, which is a drafting choice and not an actuarial one.** For the Plan 1 profile, at Pennsylvania's **4%** rate A-1 costs the veteran **$41,891** — *cheaper* than A-3's $45,850. At VRS's **6.75%** assumed return it costs **$80,442**, nearly double A-3. And for both Hybrid profiles at 6.75%, A-1's price **exceeds the entire modelled value of the credit** — the net cost to the Fund goes negative, meaning the Commonwealth charges the veteran more than the benefit is worth.

This matters because counsel ranked A-1 preferred on fiscal-note grounds. That ranking holds **only at the high interest rate** — and the high interest rate is exactly what makes A-1 unaffordable to the long-service veterans this bill exists to help. The JLC should see that trade-off before it picks a structure, not after.

**A-2 is cheapest for the member in every case and therefore costliest to the Fund in every case**, and its price is flat in age, which makes it the structure most exposed to adverse selection. New York puts the remainder expressly on participating employers (§ 1000(6)); any Virginia version would need the same sentence, and for political subdivisions that sentence is what the Commission on Local Government will read first.

**A-3 also has an administrative advantage worth quoting.** Every rate group — non-hazardous, hazardous duty, the alternate hazardous duty option, and judicial — **already has a Board-adopted approximate normal cost rate**. Striking the cliff for § 51.1-142.2 B 2 service simply extends an existing published rate in time. A-3 requires **no new rate-setting by the Board and no new actuarial product**. A-1 and A-2 both require the General Assembly to invent a price that does not currently exist in Virginia law.

> **Drafting note for the Division of Legislative Services.** The **10.01%** alternate hazardous duty option is published as a stand-alone rate with **no plan column at all**, available only to localities that have elected the option. Drafting A-3 by reference to "the approximate normal cost of the retirement plan under which the member is covered" may therefore not cleanly reach it. See Annex 2 Closure, FM-3.

### If the full fix cannot pass

Two fallbacks, in order of preference:

- **Fallback 1 — payment flexibility only (item 2 alone). Zero actuarial cost.** Keep the actuarial price, but let veterans pay it over 60 months at any point in their career. This removes the cash-flow barrier with *no* unfunded liability — only administrative cost. It is extremely hard to oppose on fiscal grounds and makes a good amendment if the price fix draws a negative impact statement.
- **Fallback 2 — a longer window instead of no window.** Extend the military window from 24 to 60 months, and give current employees a one-time 24-month open-enrollment window after the effective date. Caps the exposure while still reaching most veterans.

**Not recommended:** a free "eligibility-only" bridge credit that counts toward retirement eligibility but not the pension amount. It sounds cheaper, but it is not — the veteran pays nothing, so the plan absorbs the whole cost — and it requires inventing a new category of service credit, which multiplies the drafting risk and the chance of unintended spillover. Analysis in Annex A, § 6.
