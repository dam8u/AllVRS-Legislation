## 1. The one-line political read

**This is not a veterans bill fight — it is a 2015 cost-neutrality bargain being reopened, and the House Appropriations Subcommittee on Compensation and Retirement is the only room that matters.** The 24-month window we are removing was created by Chapter 508 of the 2015 Acts of Assembly, patroned by the then-chair of House Appropriations, for the stated purpose of making purchase of service credit "more cost-neutral to the Retirement System fund" — and it passed 99–0 and 38–0. In 2026 that same subcommittee killed 27 of the 28 VRS bills before it. The single survivor was patroned by a member of the subcommittee and was described by VRS as codifying existing VRS policy. That is the shape of the win condition.

**And one line on sequencing, added in v1.1, because it has a date on it.** Before any of that matters, the bill needs a constituency, and the body that supplies it is our own client. The JLC's last board meeting before the 2027 session is **2026-12-17** — the Council's documented occasion for taking up initiatives not voted on at the fall meeting, and for reviewing the patron list. That is the gate, and it is 53 days out.

## 2. The proposal's concrete effects

Positions follow effects, so the effects come first. Under the scope decided by JLCVSO on 2026-10-24 (all VRS members, no local opt-in, immediate access):

| Who | What they get |
|---|---|
| **Veteran VRS members past 24 months of service** | **A benefit.** Access to up to four years of military service credit at the published approximate normal cost instead of actuarial equivalent cost. The benefit is largest for long-service Plan 1 members near unreduced eligibility — precisely the cohort the pricing guardrail would charge most. |
| **The VRS trust funds (state, teacher, political subdivision)** | **A cost.** Unfunded liability from (i) the gap between approximate normal cost and actuarial equivalent cost, and (ii) retirement-date acceleration. Modelled one-time present value depending on pool and take-up — **for the figure, see the current package authority at `00-ONE-PAGE-BRIEF.md` v1.3; "$13M–$182M" is retracted.** |
| **All VRS-participating employers in each pooled plan** | **A cost, shared.** Because the plans are pooled, cost lands on employer contribution rates, not on the employers whose own veterans purchase. VRS stated this mechanic itself in 2023: "Granting of service in a pooled plan will effectively spread the cost of each period of granted service over all of the employers in the Teacher plan." |
| **School divisions and the localities that fund them** | **A cost they cannot decline.** Teacher plan rates are set statewide; political subdivisions set their own rates under § 51.1-145 but cannot opt out of a statutory change to § 51.1-142.2. |
| **The general fund** | **No appropriation ask — but not "no cost," and the distinction is now contested inside our own package.** The bill contains no appropriation clause, and VRS recorded "GF budget impacts: No immediate impact" on the closest analogue (HB 1867, 2023). But Red Team **F-2** finds that the one-pager's "General fund cost: $0 — that is structural, not a rounding" is contradicted by our own model, which assigns $20M–$37M of present value to state agencies (GF **and** NGF) and $13M–$31M to school divisions through the Standards of Quality. **I adopt the Red Team's reading for political purposes**: the defensible sentence is "this bill requests no appropriation," not "this bill costs the general fund nothing." The political cost of the broader claim is asymmetric and severe — see § 3.3 (CLG) and § 5. |
| **VRS as administrator** | **A duty, and a modest one.** Every rate group already has a Board-adopted approximate normal cost rate (Annex 2 Closure FM-3). The analogue administrative cost VRS quoted in 2023 was approximately $78,000 one-time for handbook, manual, e-course, web and employer communications revisions. |
| **The VRS Board of Trustees** | **A duty and new discretion.** Must file the § 30-19.1:7 impact statement, and under the Annex A v1.1 schedule authority would gain a rate-setting dial graded on age, creditable service and amount purchased. |
| **Employers who currently pay for purchases on behalf of employees** | **No change, but a talking point against us.** VRS noted in 2023 that "Employers currently can choose to pay for purchase of this service credit on behalf of the employee" — the existing non-legislative path, which opponents will offer as the sufficient remedy. |
| **Non-veteran members holding other purchasable categories under subdivision B 1** | **A new inequity.** Educational leave, FMLA leave, refunded service and other B 1 categories keep the 24-month cliff. This is the pressure point employee associations will push on. |

**The silent beneficiary.** The members who gain most — mid- and late-career veteran state employees, teachers and local employees — are unorganized as veterans. They are scattered across 100+ employers, they do not know they are a class, and they will not appear at a subcommittee docket. Their silence is not consent and must not be read as weak demand. The organised veteran community (§ 3.4) speaks for *veterans*, not for *veteran public employees*, and those are different constituencies with different asks.

**Two honest problems with the DHRM argument, which the subcommittee will find if we do not state them first.**

1. **The retention case points the wrong way in time.** DHRM's attrition problem is concentrated in years 0–5 — which is *inside* the existing 24-month window. A veteran who leaves in year three was always able to buy at the discounted price. Our bill helps retention in years 5–25. That is a real benefit (it gives a mid-career veteran a reason to stay for a milestone) but it is **not** the problem DHRM told Senate Finance it has, and claiming otherwise is how we lose credibility with Michael Jay and Mike Tweedy.
2. **DHRM cannot count veterans.** Its own slide reports classified employees as **6% veteran, 25% not a veteran, 69% unknown**. The denominator for any take-up estimate does not exist in DHRM's data, and VRS said the same of teachers in 2023. **This is the single most consequential operational fact in the whole political analysis** and it is addressed in § 5.

**The Dillon's Rule reading.** Virginia localities oppose preemption and unfunded mandates as a *category*, nearly independent of the policy. The useful consequence is that the objection is not about veterans and is therefore purchasable. The two things VACo's own program says it wants are (a) the Commonwealth bearing the cost, or (b) the provision not binding localities until funded. **Option (b) is functionally the opt-in-by-resolution structure modelled on § 51.1-153 B 4** that JLCVSO rejected on 2026-10-24. See § 6.

**Conflict to flag to the Fiscal & Impact Modeler.** VACo and VML will characterise this as an unfunded mandate with a cost to localities. Our model says no appropriation ask plus a one-time present value borne through employer contribution rates (figure at `00` v1.3). Both statements can be true at once, and the package already says so. But **present value is the wrong unit for this audience.** A county administrator responds to "this adds N basis points to your FY29 employer rate," not to a statewide PV range. The political analysis needs a per-employer rate-impact figure for the political subdivision pool and for the Teacher plan, at low and high take-up. **Requested of Fiscal & Impact Modeler as a discrete deliverable — partially delivered as of 2026-10-25; see § 11.** For scale, the Board-certified FY27/FY28 rates are: public school teachers 12.20%, state employees 11.07%, SPORS 31.67%, VaLORS 22.60%, JRS 30.49%; VACo reports the average actuarially determined political-subdivision DB rate at 4.98% without enhanced hazardous duty and 12.32% with it.

## 5. The real fight

**It is two actors and one provision.**

> **The fight is the House Appropriations Compensation and Retirement subcommittee, armed with a VRS impact statement that will say the take-up rate is unknowable — and the provision at issue is the price, not the deadline.** Nobody in Richmond objects to a veteran buying military service credit at age 48. They object to buying it at a price set for a 24-year-old, and to a fiscal note that no one can bound because neither VRS nor DHRM can count how many veterans are in the workforce (DHRM: 69% unknown veteran status; VRS, 2023: "not able to determine the number").

The mechanism by which we lose is precise and it has already happened once. VRS files a statement that says the per-member liability is $10,000–$60,000 and the population is undeterminable; it then builds an illustrative scenario with a round take-up assumption; the subcommittee reads the resulting total, sees no general fund ask but a pooled liability it cannot size, and lays the bill on the table 8-0 in under four minutes on an unrecorded voice vote. No one argues about veterans. That is exactly the HB 1867 transcript.

**What breaks the mechanism.** Not advocacy. A number. Specifically:

1. **Bound the cost in the bill's own text, because the denominator cannot be pre-empted.** The published range exists *because* the pool is unknown, and a range that wide is an invitation to the high end. Pricing is therefore the instrument — but **the specific version the package currently offers does not work, and Red Team F-1 is right about why.** Annex A § 2.2 requires any Board schedule to apply uniformly within a classification *and* never to exceed any individual member's actuarial equivalent cost; a uniform rate satisfying that ceiling for everyone in a class must be set at the class **minimum**, which on the team's own model recovers 25%–73% of the excess, not 100%. So "the Board can dial the fiscal note to zero" is not a sentence we can say to the money committees. Two things follow politically:
   - **Do not carry the zero-fiscal-note claim into any hearing.** If a member asks whether the Board can eliminate the cost and the answer is a uniform-rate schedule, the honest answer is "it recovers most of it in most classes," and anyone who reads our own model will find the 25% case. Making that claim and losing it is worse than never making it.
   - **Put the recovery floor in statute, and take the precedent from Ohio.** **ORC § 145.301 solves exactly this problem by statute**: no filing window at all, with cost control delivered as a hard statutory price floor — the member pays "not less than fifty per cent of the additional liability" for armed-forces active duty. A Virginia analogue (a statutory minimum percentage of actuarial equivalent cost, rather than a Board schedule constrained by an individual-member ceiling) gives the subcommittee a number it can read off the bill's face, removes the uniformity-versus-ceiling trap F-1 identifies, and is defensible as a national pattern rather than a Virginia invention. **This is the strongest single contribution my precedent research makes to the F-1 problem, and it is handed to Counsel and the Drafting Specialist as a lead, not as a legal conclusion — the drafting is theirs.**

   Either way, Annex A § 79 states the price honestly: a high recovery setting removes the pricing subsidy from exactly the long-service Plan 1 veterans most eager for the bill, leaving them access and the 60-month payment term. Policy Director and JLCVSO have to decide whether access alone is the win. **My read: it is, and it is the only version that clears this subcommittee.**
2. **Convert present value into basis points** per employer group (requested of Fiscal & Impact Modeler, § 3.3).
3. **Distinguish HB 1867 in one sentence, in the bill's own summary.** HB 1867 granted service *free*; this bill *charges* the member the published approximate normal cost. VRS's 2023 objection — "granting service with no employer or employee contributions will contribute to the unfunded liability" — does not transfer, and the subcommittee should be told that before VRS's statement arrives, not after.

## 7. Precedent: how other states handle the timing of military service credit purchase

The task asked whether other states have removed or extended time limits. **One has done so explicitly and recently, and the dominant national pattern is a service-milestone or pre-retirement trigger rather than a window measured from hire.**

| State | Statute / act | Window to purchase | Price | Relevance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Massachusetts** | **HERO Act, Chapter 178 of the Acts of 2024, § 52** (signed 2024-08-08) | **Repealed the 180-day limitation that had existed since 1996.** Members must now make full payment or enter an installment agreement **no later than one year after accumulating 10 years of creditable service, or August 8, 2025, whichever is later.** | — | **The direct precedent, and an honest one.** Massachusetts did *not* go to unlimited: it moved the trigger from a short post-eligibility window to a **service milestone**. That is a model for a Virginia concession, not for our opening draft. |
| **Ohio** | **ORC § 145.301** | **"The credit may be purchased at any time prior to receipt of a retirement allowance."** No window. | Member pays **not less than 50%** of the additional actuarial liability for armed-forces active duty; **100%** for reserve/National Guard service. Max 5 years. | **The cleanest structural precedent for our bill**: no deadline, with cost containment delivered through a statutory *price floor* rather than a *filing cliff*. This is the Ohio answer to the adverse-selection objection and it is available to Virginia through the Annex A § 2.2 schedule authority. |
| **New York** | **N.Y. Ret. & Soc. Sec. Law § 1000** | **"A member must make application for such credit before the effective date of retirement."** No window from hire. | Member pays 3% of the prior 12 months' compensation per year of credit (**6%** for members who joined on or after April 1, 2012); max **3 years**; "All costs… other than the member costs… **shall be paid by the state and all employers**." Requires **5 years of credited service** to be eligible. | Validates both halves of our structure: a pre-retirement deadline (Annex A's 12-month pre-retirement deadline is *stricter* than New York's) and an eligibility **floor** rather than a ceiling. Also the source of the brief's A-2 option — and note the employer-pays sentence, which is what the Commission on Local Government would read first. |
| **Pennsylvania** | **71 Pa.C.S. § 5304** (SERS); PSERS parallel | **No application deadline for nonintervening military service.** Max 5 years. PSERS requires **3 years of credited school service following** the military service. The statute *does* impose a one-year election window on one narrow unrelated category (temporary federal employees, 1970–1975). | Actuarial-cost basis; the brief's A-1 option turns on the statutory interest rate. | The narrow one-year window elsewhere in the same section is useful evidence: **the Pennsylvania legislature knows how to write a filing deadline and chose not to apply one to military service.** |
| **Texas** | ERS and TRS administrative practice | Purchase before retirement, but **cost escalates with interest from first eligibility** ("buy by Aug. 31 to avoid a cost increase"). | Interest accrues from first eligibility. | The **gradient** model: price rises continuously with delay instead of stepping off a cliff at 24 months. A credible Virginia fallback if the Board-set schedule is rejected as too discretionary. |

**The argument this table supports, stated for the one-pager.** Virginia is not unusual in charging a late purchaser more. Virginia is unusual in keying the price break to **elapsed time since the date of hire** — a trigger that is unrelated to the member's service, unrelated to the member's distance from retirement, and that for a veteran hired mid-career expires before the member has any realistic ability to pay. Ohio, Pennsylvania and New York all key the question to **service completed** or **proximity to retirement**; Massachusetts repealed its short window in 2024 and replaced it with a ten-year service milestone. Chapter 508 (2015) said its purpose was to bring Virginia "more in line with other states' programs." On military service credit specifically, it did the opposite.
